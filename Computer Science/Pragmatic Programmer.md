"The Pragmatic Programmer" by David Thomas and Andrew Hunt is a book, first released in October 1999, designed to help promote an outlook on programming very intrinsically tied to Agile Programming. Below are my notes on the subject.

## What is a Pragmatic Programmer?

The Pragmatic Programmer is a philosophy. The central tenets are:
1. **It is your life.** You can affect the world. You only have one life. Why not?
2. **There is always context.** A problem can be magnified and distilled down to a single issue, but there is always a larger view that can give you the context and motivation behind the issue. 
3. **Take responsibility.** You have the power to make changes. You may be the only person able to do so. Do it.

From these core tenets, there is much that follows:
- **Instigate change.**
- **Be at least as good as you need to be.**
- **Always be developing your Knowledge Portfolio.**
- **Learn how to communicate.**

## Tips

### 1. Care about your craft

If you do something, do it right. Take the time to understand what you are doing.

### 2. Think! about your work

Think. All the time. About everything you work on. Never run on auto-pilot. Constantly ask yourself - Is this the best way forward?

### 3. You have agency

If something is less-than-ideal in your personal-work balance, ask about it. If you feel like you are being passed by, then do something about it. Learn in your free time.

### 4. Provide options, not excuses

There are absolutely situations in which a thing cannot be done. That said, many times, individuals will hit road blocks on projects because of mental barriers, not because of real blocks. 

When you hit a road block, ask yourself if the block is real, or just requires a different avenue of investigation.  Is there something else you can try, a new prototype or even deleting code? Is the problem you? If so, figure it out! If you are stuck, ask for help! Always look to salvage a situation, not abandon it.

### 5. Don't live with broken windows.

When you see something that should be repaired, do it. This will keep software rot from eating your projects alive... immediately. If it cannot be repaired immediately, board it up - communicate with others that it is being worked on. On a related note, when you go to fix something, make sure you aren't damaging others. **Do no harm**.

### 6. Be a catalyst for change

If you see what is needed, you may be the only person to realize it. Get it done before it gets forgotten. If you have trouble convincing others, start with something small.

### 7. Remember the big picture

Be aware of what other people are doing. Everyone is working and changing things. Without guidance, a bunch of small changes can lead to big change, not necessarily a good change.

### 8. Make quality a requirements issue

Requirements change frequently. It is better to deliver a straightforward program quicker, then wait to release a 'perfect' one later, as the idea of what a 'perfect' program *is* may change by the time it is released.

### 9. Invest regularly in your knowledge portfolio

Making habits is important. It is great to spend 6 hours on a project in a week, but it would be better to spend 1 hour each day instead.  Your knowledge portfolio is a collection of the things you know, and the tools in your metaphorical toolbelt. Add tools habitually, and you will be better for it.

### 10. Critically analyze what you read and hear

Critical thinking is important. Some tips to help:
- Ask the five "why"s
- Who does this benefit?
- What's the context?
- When/where would this work?
- Why is this a problem?

### 11. English is just another programming language

Our communication is intended to achieve a result. As such, it can be considered a programming language. Try implementing some programming methodologies in the way you communicate. Try not to repeat yourself, try to build your communication with an eye towards extensibility. 

### 12. It's both what you say and the way you say it

Great content cannot alone make great communication. The form is important, too.

### 13. Build documentation in, don't bolt it on

Comments in code can be very important, but you should not comment everything. The more comments you write, the more likely that those comments will end up referring to code that gets removed, or modified beyond recognition. Only comment when there is something pertinent that is not immediately visible.
